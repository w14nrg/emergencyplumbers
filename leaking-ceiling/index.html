Ceiling leaks

Water coming through the ceiling

A drip, a spreading stain or a bulging ceiling all mean water is collecting above you. The first job is working out where it's coming from — and whether it's yours or the flat upstairs.

Do this now

  1. If the ceiling is sagging or bulging, keep everyone out from underneath it.
  2. Keep away from wet light fittings. Only switch off the lighting circuit at the consumer unit if you can reach it safely.
  3. If there's a bathroom or kitchen above you in your own home, turn off your stopcock.
  4. If it's coming from a flat above, knock or call them and ask them to stop using water and turn off their stopcock.
  5. Put down buckets and towels, move furniture, and photograph the damage.
If water is near sockets, light fittings or the consumer unit, don't touch them. Only switch the electricity off at the consumer unit if you can reach it without standing in water or touching anything wet. If you can't, keep everyone away from that area and get an electrician. If the power supply itself looks damaged, the free 105 line reaches your local electricity network.

Where is the water coming from?

How the leak behaves tells us a lot about its source. These are the four we see most:

Pressurised supply leak

Constant, whether or not anyone is using water, and doesn't stop at night. Usually a hot or cold supply pipe or fitting. Turning off the stopcock slows it within minutes.

Waste or overflow leak

Only happens when a bath, shower, basin or toilet above is used, then stops. Usually a waste joint, a failed seal around a shower tray or bath, or a leaking toilet connection.

Heating leak

Warm or discoloured water, often near radiator pipes, and the boiler pressure keeps dropping. Usually a radiator valve, a joint or a pinhole in heating pipework.

From another property

Water coming from the flat above, a shared riser, a communal tank or the roof. Your stopcock won't stop it, and you'll need access to the other property.

Rainwater coming in through a roof, gutter or window is a roofing problem rather than plumbing. If the leak only appears during heavy rain, tell us — we'll say if it's worth a plumber attending.

What we do when we arrive

  1. Stop or slow the water. Isolate the supply, the appliance or the heating system — or get access to the property above.
  2. Trace the source. We check the room above, run water through individual fittings one at a time, and look at the pattern of wetness. In converted houses the water can travel along joists and appear well away from the actual leak.
  3. Repair what's reachable. Waste joints, toilet connections, valves and exposed pipework can usually be fixed on the visit. Concealed pipes may mean lifting a floorboard or removing a bath or shower panel.
  4. Explain what's left. If the cause is behind tiles or inside a wall, we isolate it and explain the options.

When the leak is from upstairs

If the water is coming from a neighbour's flat and you can't get access, we can still help: we'll check whether it's your pipework, confirm the source is above, and where possible isolate the supply to that flat from a communal valve. In mansion blocks the porter or managing agent can often give access to a riser or to the flat itself. Your building insurance or the freeholder may be responsible for leaks from communal pipes.

A bulging ceiling

A ceiling that is sagging with water can come down suddenly. Keep people and pets out from underneath. If it's a small, localised bulge and nowhere near a light fitting, some people relieve the pressure with a small hole into a bucket — but if the plaster is cracking, sagging widely or near electrics, stay away and wait for help.

Tracing a ceiling leak usually takes longer than fixing a visible burst, because the source isn't always where the water appears.

Questions

Who pays if the leak is from the flat above?

Usually the owner of the pipe or appliance that leaked, or their insurer — but leases and building policies vary. Photograph everything and keep a note of when it started. We can give you a written note of what we found.

Will you need to cut into my ceiling?

Not usually. We trace leaks from above wherever we can. Cutting a ceiling is only needed if the leak is inside the ceiling void and can't be reached from the floor above.

Can a ceiling leak wait until morning?

If you've turned off the water and the drip has stopped, it may be able to wait. If water is still coming through, the ceiling is bulging, or it's near electrics, call us now.
